"AI" gets used to describe everything from a chatbot to a self-driving car
to the autocomplete on your phone, which makes it feel like a single
mysterious thing. It isn't. [Artificial intelligence](glossary:artificial-intelligence)
is a broad field: it's any system that performs tasks we'd normally say
require human intelligence — recognizing a face in a photo, translating a
sentence, recommending a song you'll probably like.

## How is this different from regular software?

Ordinary software runs on rules a person wrote down explicitly. A tax
calculator applies the tax code line by line, exactly as a programmer typed
it. If the output is wrong, you go find the line of code that's wrong and
fix it.

[Machine learning](glossary:machine-learning) — the approach behind almost
everything people mean when they say "AI" today — works differently. Instead
of a programmer writing explicit rules, a model is shown a large number of
examples and learns patterns from them on its own. Show a model thousands
of photos labeled "cat" or "not cat," and it gradually gets better at
telling the difference — without anyone writing a rule like "cats have
pointy ears."

This distinction matters because it changes how these systems fail. A
traditional program's bugs are usually specific and fixable. A machine
learning model's mistakes are usually statistical: it will be wrong some
percentage of the time, in ways that are hard to predict in advance and
hard to fix by editing a single line.

## A few things AI is not

- **It's not one technology.** "AI" spans simple statistical models,
  large-scale [machine learning](glossary:machine-learning) systems, and
  everything in between. A recommendation engine and a chatbot are both
  "AI," and they work in almost entirely different ways.
- **It's not new.** The field dates to the 1950s. What changed recently is
  the amount of data and computing power available, which made
  dramatically more capable systems possible.
- **It's not understanding in the way a person understands.** Modern AI
  systems are extremely good at finding patterns in data. Whether that
  constitutes "understanding" is a genuinely open question, and it's worth
  being skeptical of confident claims in either direction.

## Why this matters for you

Whatever your role, the practical skill worth building isn't "know how to
build an AI system" (unless that's literally your job) — it's "know enough
to reason about what a given AI system can plausibly do, and where it's
likely to be unreliable." That's a learnable skill, and it's what the rest
of this path is built around.
